# Talk to AI Like a Capable Assistant — and Know When It Is Lying

**Read time: 12–15 minutes · Task: 10 minutes**

Yesterday you met the three ideas: real material, audience, format. Today we turn them into a structure you can reuse every single time — and we look honestly at what to do when the AI lies to you. It does lie, confidently, and today you will watch it happen.

## Why one-line questions get one-line answers

Type "write a memo about punctuality" and you get a generic memo — correct grammar, no connection to your office, your boss, or the actual incident. The AI had nothing to work with, so it produced the most average answer in existence.

The fix is a structure. Call it **R-C-F**:

- **R — Role.** Tell it who it should be. "You are an experienced admin officer in a Nigerian state ministry." A role sets vocabulary, tone and judgement.
- **C — Context.** Tell it the situation. Who the output is for, what happened, what you already know. This is where you paste your real material.
- **F — Format.** Tell it the shape of the answer. "A one-page memo with three numbered sections." Bullets, a table, a short paragraph — say it.

## Before and after

**Asked badly:**

> write about our late-coming problem

Result: a schoolboy essay about punctuality in general. Useless.

**Asked well (R-C-F):**

```
ROLE: You are an experienced admin officer in a Nigerian state ministry.

CONTEXT: Staff in my department resume late, especially on Mondays.
I have written two reminder circulars already and nothing changed.
This memo is for my Head of Department, who can approve real action.

FORMAT: A short memo: subject line, one paragraph stating the problem
with its effect on work, then three numbered options for action,
each with one sentence on its cost or disruption.
```

Result: a memo that reads like it came from someone who knows the office.

## When AI lies

Here is a reproducible example you can run yourself. Ask ChatGPT or Gemini:

```
What percentage of Nigerian adults have a bank account? Cite your source.
```

It will answer confidently — a specific number, sometimes even a "source". Run that check yourself: search for the actual figure from the CBN or EFInA. The AI's number is frequently wrong, and its "source" may not exist at all.

This is not a malfunction. The AI predicts likely-sounding text; it does not check facts. Experts call it *hallucination*. You should call it what it is: **a confident guess.** The more specific the number or citation, the more you must verify it.

## The 4-question check

Run these on every output before you use it:

1. **Does it name a source?** If no source is given, treat every claim as unverified.
2. **Does the source exist?** Search for it. Invented citations are the classic AI lie.
3. **Do the numbers add up?** Check totals, percentages and dates against what you know.
4. **Would I say this to my boss with my name on it?** If you hesitate at any sentence, rewrite or delete it.

## Data privacy, plainly

**Never paste into a free AI tool:** salaries or payroll data, patient or customer details with names, unpublished documents, anything marked confidential, anyone's personal information.

**Safe to paste:** notes you wrote yourself, publicly available documents (published policies, public reports), and text where names and identifying details are removed.

You are sending that text to another company's computers. Send it nothing you would not pin on a public notice board — anonymised first.

## 🎯 Task

Two parts today:

1. **Rewrite one poor prompt from your own work using R-C-F.** Take a request you recently asked AI (or would ask) in one line, rewrite it with Role, Context and Format, and run both versions. Compare the two answers side by side.
2. **Catch one lie.** Ask the AI a specific, checkable question in your field — a statistic, a regulation, a date. Verify its answer using the 4-question check. Find the error and write down what it got wrong.

## 📤 Output

Your R-C-F rewrite (keep the better answer) and one caught error, written down in your own words. Both go into your Playbook on Day 7 — the caught error is worth more than ten good answers, because it taught you distrust.
