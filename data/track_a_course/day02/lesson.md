# Talk to AI Like a Capable Assistant, and Know When It Is Wrong

**Read time: 12–15 minutes · Task: 10 minutes**

Yesterday you met the three ideas: real material, audience and format. Today you turn them into a structure you can reuse every time. You will also see how AI can state something wrong with complete confidence, and learn what to do about it.

## Why one-line questions get one-line answers

Type "write a memo about punctuality" and you get a generic memo: correct grammar, but no connection to your office, your boss or what happened. The AI had nothing to work with, so it produced the most average answer it could.

The fix is a structure. Call it **R-C-F**:

- **R: Role.** Tell it who it should be: "You are an experienced admin officer in a Nigerian state ministry." A role sets vocabulary, tone and judgement.
- **C: Context.** Tell it the situation: who the output is for, what happened and what you already know. This is where you paste your real material.
- **F: Format.** Tell it the shape of the answer: "A one-page memo with three numbered sections." Bullets, a table or a short paragraph, say which.

## Before and after

**Asked badly:**

> write about our late-coming problem

Result: a school essay about punctuality in general, which you cannot use.

**Asked well (R-C-F):**

```
ROLE: You are an experienced admin officer in a Nigerian state ministry.

CONTEXT: Staff in my department resume late, especially on Mondays.
I have written two reminder circulars already and nothing changed.
This memo is for my Head of Department, who can approve real action.

FORMAT: A short memo: subject line, one paragraph stating the problem
with its effect on work, then three numbered options for action,
each with one sentence on its cost or disruption.
```

Result: a memo that reads as if it came from someone who knows the office.

## When AI gets it wrong

Here is an example you can try yourself. Ask ChatGPT or Gemini:

```
What percentage of Nigerian adults have a bank account? Cite your source.
```

It will answer confidently, with a specific number and sometimes a named source. Now check it: search for the figure in the original publication from the CBN or EFInA, and compare the year and the definition used (for example, "banked" can mean different things in different surveys). The AI's answer may turn out to be correct, outdated, unsupported or wrong. Do not assume either way until you have checked.

AI can produce text that sounds right but is not accurate. Some services can search the web, but that still does not make every answer reliable. Experts call this *hallucination*. A plainer name is **a confident guess**. The more specific the number or citation, the more carefully you must check it.

## The 4-question check

Run these on every output before you use it:

1. **Does it name a source?** If it gives no source, treat every claim as unverified.
2. **Does the source exist?** Search for it. Invented citations are one of the most common AI errors.
3. **Do the numbers add up?** Check totals, percentages and dates against what you know.
4. **Would I say this to my boss with my name on it?** If you hesitate over any sentence, rewrite or delete it.

## Data privacy, plainly

**Never paste into a free AI tool:** salaries or payroll data, patient or customer details with names, unpublished documents, anything marked confidential, or anyone's personal information.

**Safe to paste:** notes you wrote yourself, publicly available documents (published policies, public reports), and text with names and identifying details removed.

When you paste, you are sending that text to another company's computers. Do not send anything you would not pin on a public notice board, and remove names first.

## 🎯 Task

There are two parts today.

1. **Rewrite one weak prompt from your own work using R-C-F.**
   - Pick a one-line request you have asked AI recently (or would ask).
   - Rewrite it with a Role, Context and Format.
   - Send the one-line version and the R-C-F version, each in its own new chat, and compare the two answers.
2. **Verify one claim.**
   - Ask the AI a specific, checkable question from your field, such as a statistic, a regulation or a date.
   - Check its answer with the 4-question check, using the original source (an official website or publication), not the AI.
   - Write down whether the answer was supported, unsupported, outdated or incorrect, and what evidence you found.

## 📤 Output

Save two things in your notes app or a document: your R-C-F prompt with the better of the two answers, and your verification record in your own words (the question, the AI's answer, the source you checked and your verdict). Both go into your Playbook on Day 7, where the verification record shows that you checked the answer rather than trusting it.

---
**How careful users work:** they rely on the structure to get good drafts, and they check the facts themselves before using them. Keep those two jobs separate.
