**Week 1 · About 15 to 20 hours · Competency 1: Indicators**

Good analysis starts long before the data arrives. This week you'll learn how a programme is supposed to create change, and how to define the indicators that will show whether it does.

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain monitoring, evaluation, accountability and learning, and how they differ
- Build a Theory of Change with assumptions
- Turn it into a results matrix of outputs, outcomes and indicators
- Write indicator reference sheets with clear numerators, denominators, sources and frequency

## Key ideas

**MEAL.** Monitoring tracks what's happening as the project runs. Evaluation judges whether it worked and why. Accountability means participants can give feedback and see it acted on. Learning means using all of this to improve.

**The results chain.**

| Level | Meaning | Rise example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inputs | Resources | Trainers, venues, ₦ budget, starter kits |
| Activities | What the project does | 12-week trade training; business skills sessions |
| Outputs | What it directly delivers | 600 youth complete training; 540 starter kits distributed |
| Outcomes | Changes for participants | Graduates working in their trade; higher monthly earnings |
| Impact | Long-term, wider change | Reduced youth unemployment in target communities |

**Theory of Change.** The "if... then... because..." story linking activities to outcomes, with the **assumptions** that must hold. *If* youth gain trade skills and a starter kit, *then* more will earn from their trade, *because* there is local demand, *assuming* they can access a workspace and customers.

**Indicators.** A measurable sign of change. A good indicator has a precise definition, a numerator, a denominator, a data source, a frequency and a disaggregation (for example by sex, age group and state).

> **New word: denominator.** The "out of how many" in a percentage. Change the denominator and the same numerator tells a very different story.

## Worked example

```
INDICATOR REFERENCE SHEET
Indicator:      % of graduates working in any paid activity 6 months after training
Level:          Outcome
Definition:     "Working" = earned any income in the past 30 days from employment or
                self-employment. "Graduate" = attended at least 80% of sessions and
                passed the practical test.
Numerator:      Graduates due for 6-month follow-up who report working
Denominator:    All graduates due for 6-month follow-up in the period
                (non-responders reported separately; headline uses respondents, with
                response rate shown beside it)
Data source:    6-month follow-up phone survey (KoboToolbox)
Frequency:      Quarterly, for each cohort reaching 6 months
Disaggregation: Sex, age group (18–24, 25–35), state, trade
Baseline/target: Baseline 22% (intake survey); target 60%
Limitations:    Self-reported; phone non-response may bias upward
Owner:          MEAL officer
```

## Use AI well this week

AI is useful for drafting a first Theory of Change and spotting missing assumptions. It doesn't know your programme, so check every link against the project documents.

```
Here is a summary of a youth livelihoods project (no personal data). Draft a
Theory of Change as "if... then... because..." statements from activities to
outcomes. List the assumptions at each step and say which are riskiest.
Then suggest one outcome indicator per outcome, with a clear denominator.
```

> **Stay safe:** Accountability starts with design. Include at least one indicator or mechanism for participant feedback, such as a complaints line and how responses are tracked.

## Guided practice

> **Try it:** Using the Rise project summary your mentor shares (or your partner project), draw the Theory of Change, build a results matrix with at least three outputs and three outcomes, and write full reference sheets for three indicators, including one for participant feedback.

## Your evidence this week

Hand in, as described in the week 1 evidence brief:

1. A **Theory of Change** with assumptions marked
2. A **results matrix** linking activities, outputs, outcomes and indicators
3. At least three **indicator reference sheets**

What the assessor looks for: a clear distinction between outputs and outcomes, realistic assumptions, and indicators with precise definitions and defensible denominators.

## Before you hand in

- [ ] Every outcome has at least one indicator
- [ ] Every indicator has a numerator, denominator, source, frequency and disaggregation
- [ ] Outputs and outcomes aren't mixed up
- [ ] Assumptions are written down, with the riskiest marked
- [ ] At least one indicator covers accountability to participants
